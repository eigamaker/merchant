# Merchan Project

## Current Game Design Context / Concept Draft

> Status: Concept development in progress
> Purpose: Share the current design direction with Codex before detailed specification and implementation planning.

---

# 1. Project Concept

**Merchan** is a 2D RPG / merchant simulation game centered around a merchant living beside a growing dungeon.

The protagonist is not a legendary warrior.

He is an ordinary merchant with limited combat ability.

However, he has a stronger desire than anyone else to see the dungeon completely conquered.

The fundamental idea is:

> **The protagonist cannot conquer the dungeon alone, so he uses commerce, money, information, equipment, investment, relationships, and other people to make the conquest possible.**

The player therefore influences dungeon progression both:

* directly, by entering shallower sections of the dungeon and gathering materials
* indirectly, by supporting adventurers capable of reaching deeper floors

The game should create the feeling that:

> **The player is not the hero of the adventure, but is one of the main reasons the heroes can succeed.**

---

# 2. Core Theme

The strongest thematic direction identified so far is:

> **How far are you willing to endanger other people in pursuit of your own goal?**

The protagonist sincerely believes that conquering the dungeon is necessary.

However, achieving that objective requires:

* sending adventurers into dangerous areas
* supplying increasingly powerful weapons
* financing expeditions
* encouraging stronger adventurers to come to town
* increasing the scale of the town
* introducing dangerous items and technologies into society

As dungeon conquest progresses, other people may:

* become heroes
* become rich
* become injured
* die
* resent the protagonist
* appreciate the protagonist
* suffer indirectly from the protagonist's decisions

The game should generally avoid explicitly telling the player:

> "This was the good choice."

or:

> "This was the evil choice."

Instead, the game should show consequences.

---

# 3. Background of the Town and Dungeon

The protagonist was born in a small, quiet rural town.

Near the town was originally an ordinary cave.

As a child, the protagonist often explored the cave with a close childhood friend.

In this world, mysterious dungeons occasionally appear somewhere in the world.

Their exact origin is not yet finalized.

A dungeon can suddenly transform:

* caves
* ruins
* underground structures
* possibly other locations

into massive supernatural spaces.

Once a dungeon appears, it remains until someone reaches and conquers its deepest level.

After being conquered, the dungeon disappears or becomes inactive.

Eventually, another dungeon will emerge somewhere else in the world.

---

# 4. The Childhood Incident

While the protagonist and the childhood friend were exploring the cave, a dungeon suddenly formed around them.

The cave transformed.

The structure changed.

Unknown creatures appeared.

The protagonist escaped.

The childhood friend did not.

The protagonist believes the dungeon incident may have been his fault.

One possible detail:

The protagonist discovered or touched an unusual object immediately before the dungeon appeared.

Because of this, he has carried a lifelong belief such as:

> "If I hadn't touched it, none of this would have happened."

Whether this belief is objectively correct should remain uncertain for much of the game.

The childhood friend is presumed dead or lost inside the dungeon.

At the current design stage, the preferred direction is:

**The childhood friend should probably NOT simply be alive at the bottom waiting to be rescued.**

The emotional core should instead involve accepting that some things cannot be restored.

---

# 5. Protagonist Motivation

The protagonist carries guilt from the incident.

His initial emotional objective is essentially:

> **Destroy the dungeon and restore the peaceful town that existed before it appeared.**

He does not have the power to conquer the dungeon himself.

Therefore:

> "If I cannot reach the bottom myself, I will help someone else reach it."

This becomes the foundation of his life as a merchant.

---

# 6. Central Irony

This is currently one of the most important ideas in the project.

The protagonist wants:

> **the dungeon to disappear and the old peaceful town to return.**

But everything he does to achieve dungeon conquest causes exactly the opposite transformation.

To conquer the dungeon, the player needs:

* more adventurers
* stronger adventurers
* better equipment
* larger markets
* better infrastructure
* more merchants
* more information
* more investment

As a result, the rural town grows.

For example:

Small rural town

→ Adventurers arrive

→ Inn expands

→ Tavern opens

→ Blacksmith appears

→ Adventurer guild opens

→ More merchants arrive

→ Population increases

→ Wealth increases

→ Strong adventurers arrive

→ Town develops into a dungeon economy

Eventually the protagonist realizes:

> **Trying to restore the old town has destroyed the possibility of the old town ever returning.**

Even if the dungeon disappears, time cannot be reversed.

---

# 7. Town Growth Should Not Be Purely Negative

Avoid the simplistic structure:

> countryside = good
> development = bad

The growing town should produce both benefits and problems.

Positive consequences may include:

* improved medical care
* better roads
* greater employment
* higher incomes
* schools
* new shops
* improved logistics
* access to rare goods
* stronger protection

Negative consequences may include:

* rising rents
* crime
* adventurer violence
* gambling
* black markets
* displacement of old residents
* inequality
* cultural change
* overcrowding

The player should sometimes wonder:

> "Was the old town really better?"

---

# 8. Dungeon Economy

The dungeon becomes the economic engine of the town.

Dungeon materials may include:

* ores
* monster parts
* herbs
* magical materials
* weapons
* artifacts
* unidentified relics
* unique items

The protagonist gathers some personally.

Adventurers also return with materials and items.

The protagonist can:

* buy from adventurers
* sell equipment to adventurers
* sell supplies
* commission expeditions
* finance adventurers
* distribute information
* trade materials
* potentially process or manufacture goods later

The player's shop therefore becomes connected directly to dungeon progression.

---

# 9. Core Gameplay Relationship

The current high-level loop is:

**Dungeon exploration**

→ obtain materials / information

→ sell or process goods

→ earn money

→ invest in town / adventurers / equipment

→ stronger adventurers are attracted or developed

→ adventurers reach deeper dungeon floors

→ new materials and information become available

→ local economy grows

→ stronger adventurers arrive

→ deeper dungeon progression

→ repeat

The dungeon, economy, town, and adventurers should form one connected system rather than separate minigames.

---

# 10. Adventurer Ecosystem

Adventurers should exist independently of the player.

The player is not directly controlling all adventurers.

Adventurers and adventuring parties should:

* form parties
* enter the dungeon
* succeed
* fail
* become injured
* die
* retire
* become famous
* become stronger
* change relationships
* potentially leave town

The player influences them through commerce and investment.

Examples:

* sell equipment
* sell medicine
* provide credit
* lend equipment
* sponsor expeditions
* provide information
* offer discounts
* prioritize particular adventurers
* invest directly in promising adventurers

A poor young adventurer could eventually become one of the greatest adventurers in the world because the player supported them early.

Alternatively, the player may dismiss that adventurer as a bad investment.

---

# 11. Adventurer Progress News

Dungeon progress should be visible to the player even when the protagonist is not participating.

Example notifications:

> "Silver Hawk reached Floor 23."

> "Red Fang Party withdrew from Floor 31."

> "Adventurer Mina discovered an unknown mineral on Floor 18."

> "The Blue Wolves lost one member on Floor 27."

> "For the first time in history, Floor 40 has been reached."

This is important for creating the feeling:

> **The world continues moving without the protagonist physically being there.**

Ideally the player sometimes recognizes:

> "That's the sword I sold them."

or:

> "I financed that expedition."

This connects commerce to the adventure narrative.

---

# 12. Merchant Responsibility

One of the main philosophical/gameplay ideas is:

> **The value of an item is not simply its selling price.**

A merchant normally thinks:

Buy cheaply
→ sell for more
→ make profit.

Merchan should complicate this.

The important question becomes:

> **Who are you selling this item to?**

The player is not merely moving inventory.

Items can change people and the world.

---

# 13. Item Consequence System

Working system name:

## Item Consequence System

Items, especially rare or important ones, can generate consequences after leaving the player's shop.

Conceptual formula:

> **Item × Owner × Situation = Consequence**

Example:

The protagonist discovers a strange sword.

Initial information:

**Old Black Sword**

* Attack: high
* Estimated value: 4,500G
* Origin: unknown

The player sells it to an adventurer.

Several days later:

> "Twelve citizens killed in the northern district."

The attacker is eventually identified.

They are carrying:

**the sword sold by the player.**

The protagonist did nothing directly except sell an item.

Yet that transaction helped cause a major event.

This should be one of Merchan's signature mechanics.

---

# 14. Uncertainty and Appraisal

The game should NOT immediately tell players all dangerous item properties.

Bad example:

> "WARNING: Selling this sword has a 60% chance of causing a massacre."

That removes ambiguity.

Instead, knowledge about an item should depend on investigation.

Possible information levels:

### Unidentified

Old Black Sword

### Basic appraisal

Old Black Sword
High-quality weapon
Unusual magical reaction

### Advanced appraisal

The blade appears to react to blood.

### Expert appraisal

Prolonged use may affect the emotional state of its wielder.

The player can choose:

* sell immediately
* pay for appraisal
* research
* hold the item
* destroy it
* give it to authorities
* sell it to a specific adventurer
* sell it through the black market

More information costs:

* money
* time
* access
* relationships

---

# 15. "I Only Sold It"

This should become a recurring moral idea.

After a disaster:

Protagonist:

> "I didn't kill anyone. I only sold him the sword."

One NPC may agree.

Another may respond:

> "Maybe. But you were the one who brought that sword into this town."

The game should not explicitly resolve the argument.

The player should decide how responsible the protagonist is.

---

# 16. Positive Item Consequences

Item consequences must not only be negative.

Otherwise the optimal strategy becomes:

> never sell suspicious items.

Examples of positive consequences:

A cheap sword sold to a poor beginner

→ saves them in battle

→ adventurer becomes stronger

→ eventually becomes a famous hero

A healing potion

→ saves an adventurer

→ that adventurer later discovers a deeper floor

A rare tool

→ allows a craftsman to create a new industry

A book

→ inspires a child who later becomes an important researcher

The same principle applies:

> **The player may only discover the importance of a transaction much later.**

---

# 17. Item History

Important items should ideally have persistent histories.

Conceptual example:

**Blooddrinker Sword #00421**

Discovered by protagonist on Floor 17
→ appraised in town
→ sold to Gareth for 3,800G
→ Gareth killed an Ogre on Floor 21
→ Gareth reached Floor 24
→ Gareth's mental condition deteriorated
→ tavern fight
→ two guards killed
→ city massacre incident

The full history does not need to be shown immediately.

The player can learn it through:

* news
* NPC conversations
* rumors
* investigation
* newspapers / bulletin boards
* adventurer reports

This makes the world feel causally connected.

---

# 18. Scale of Item Consequences

Not every item should create dramatic events.

Consequences should exist at multiple scales.

Small:

Selling cheap food
→ struggling adventurer eats properly

Medium:

Selling good armor
→ party survival rate improves

Large:

Mass production of powerful weapons
→ dungeon progress accelerates
→ violent crime also increases

Extreme:

Selling a cursed artifact
→ major town catastrophe

This prevents the system from feeling artificial.

---

# 19. World Impact Items

Implementation concept:

Normal items can use systemic tags and statistical effects.

Examples:

* weapon
* healing
* cursed
* addictive
* magical
* prestige
* prohibited
* religious
* volatile

A smaller number of special items can be:

## World Impact Items

These have bespoke event chains.

This keeps implementation manageable while allowing memorable authored events.

---

# 20. Player Choices Around Dangerous Items

Possible actions for significant items:

* sell
* refuse to sell
* store
* destroy
* investigate
* lend
* donate
* give to authorities
* give to an adventurer
* sell to black market
* auction
* dismantle

No action should automatically be the morally correct one.

Example:

Giving a dangerous artifact to the kingdom may seem responsible.

But the kingdom could later use it in war.

Destroying the artifact might prevent a disaster.

But perhaps an adventurer needed it to survive the deepest dungeon floor.

Consequences should often be uncertain.

---

# 21. Post-Dungeon Economy

An important long-term concept:

If conquering the dungeon causes it to disappear, then dungeon conquest also removes the town's primary economic engine.

Therefore:

> **The protagonist may succeed in destroying the dungeon while simultaneously destroying the economy they created.**

During the game, the player may choose whether to diversify the economy.

Possible alternative industries:

* agriculture
* manufacturing
* education
* tourism
* crafts
* trade
* logistics
* research

A town completely dependent on dungeon activity may collapse after conquest.

A diversified town may survive.

Again, the game should present this as a consequence rather than explicitly labeling endings good or bad.

---

# 22. Pet System

A separate but emotionally important feature:

The protagonist eventually gains a pet.

Possible origin:

A stray dog or cat begins following the protagonist around town.

The relationship begins naturally rather than through a formal quest.

At first the pet may:

* sleep near the shop
* follow the protagonist
* steal food
* react to customers
* appear in everyday scenes

The player can:

* name it
* feed it
* interact with it
* gradually form attachment

Eventually the pet follows the protagonist into the dungeon.

---

# 23. Pet Rescue Gameplay

The pet should not merely be cosmetic.

If the protagonist collapses inside the dungeon:

**control transfers to the pet.**

The gameplay changes.

The pet cannot defeat monsters normally.

Possible actions:

* run
* hide
* track scents
* bark / call for help
* navigate toward known adventurers
* return toward the entrance

If the pet successfully reaches help:

adventurers may rescue the protagonist.

This system should occur more than once.

The player may therefore psychologically begin relying on the pet:

> "I can take a little more risk because my dog can get help."

This creates mechanical attachment rather than relying only on scripted emotional scenes.

---

# 24. Pet Death / Loss

The pet should NOT necessarily be scripted to die in every playthrough.

Possible outcomes should emerge from situations and player decisions.

Examples:

* pet reaches help
* pet becomes injured
* protagonist dies but pet survives
* pet dies attempting rescue
* both die
* adventurers rescue both

One possible dramatic event:

The protagonist collapses deep underground.

Control switches to the pet.

The pet runs through dangerous areas trying to reach help.

The pet may fail and be killed.

The protagonist may then also die.

The emotional power should come from the fact that the player has relied on and interacted with the pet throughout the game.

---

# 25. Narrative Principle: Attachment Through Gameplay

Important design principle:

> **Do not tell the player that a character is important. Make the character important through repeated gameplay.**

This should apply to:

* pets
* adventurers
* shop customers
* town residents
* business partners

Player attachment should emerge from:

* transactions
* repeated interactions
* favors
* shared history
* consequences

rather than long exposition alone.

---

# 26. Relationship to Undertale

Undertale is a design influence, but Merchan should NOT imitate its surface features.

Do NOT simply reproduce:

* pacifist combat
* quirky monsters
* fourth-wall jokes
* Undertale-style morality routes

The relevant lesson is:

> **Take a normal game mechanic and make the player reconsider what that mechanic means.**

Undertale questions:

> "Why do RPG players kill enemies for EXP?"

Merchan should question:

> "What responsibility does a merchant have for what happens after a sale?"

and:

> "If your goal is important enough, how much danger are you willing to impose on other people?"

Commerce itself should become narrative.

---

# 27. Major Tensions

Merchan should repeatedly place the player between competing objectives.

### Profit

Make money.

### Dungeon Progress

Help adventurers reach deeper floors.

### Human Safety

Keep adventurers and citizens alive.

### Town Prosperity

Grow the local economy.

### Original Goal

Destroy the dungeon.

These objectives should frequently conflict.

For example:

A powerful but unstable adventurer wants an extremely strong weapon.

Selling it:

* large profit
* faster dungeon progression

- increased danger

Refusing:

* lost profit
* slower dungeon progression

- possibly safer town

There should rarely be an obviously correct answer.

---

# 28. Current Core Statement

Current working definition of Merchan:

> **A merchant who lost a childhood friend when a dungeon appeared beneath his rural hometown spends his life trying to destroy that dungeon. Unable to conquer it himself, he builds an economy capable of attracting and supporting increasingly powerful adventurers. In doing so, he transforms the quiet town he hoped to restore, risks the lives of countless others, and gradually discovers that his ordinary commercial decisions can change the fate of individuals and the entire town.**

---

# 29. Emotional Arc

Initial belief:

> "If I destroy the dungeon, things can return to the way they were."

Mid-game:

> "I need stronger adventurers. I need more money. I need this town to grow."

Later:

The protagonist begins seeing the costs of that obsession.

* deaths
* damaged relationships
* crime
* economic dependence
* town transformation
* consequences of sold items

End-game realization:

> **Destroying the dungeon cannot restore the past.**

The childhood friend is gone.

The original town is gone.

The protagonist's childhood is gone.

The real question becomes:

> **What should be preserved, and what kind of future should be created after the dungeon disappears?**

---

# 30. Important Current Design Principles

1. **The protagonist is a merchant first, not a disguised warrior hero.**

2. **Dungeon conquest should happen partly through other people.**

3. **The world continues moving independently of the protagonist.**

4. **Transactions create consequences.**

5. **Items can have histories after being sold.**

6. **Avoid explicit good/evil meters whenever possible.**

7. **Show consequences rather than judging the player.**

8. **Player attachment should emerge through gameplay.**

9. **Town growth must have both positive and negative consequences.**

10. **Dungeon conquest, commerce, adventurers, and town development must be one interconnected system.**

11. **The player's original objective and the systems needed to achieve it should fundamentally conflict.**

12. **Loss cannot simply be undone at the end of the story.**

# 33. Instruction for Codex

For now, treat this document as:

**game design context, not final implementation specification.**

Do not assume every mechanic described above is approved for implementation.

The immediate objective is to continue refining:

* story
* gameplay
* features
* game systems
* interactions between systems

Once those are stabilized, convert them into concrete technical specifications and an implementation plan.

When evaluating new ideas, prioritize:

1. Does it reinforce the merchant identity?
2. Does it connect commerce to dungeon progression?
3. Can player actions create visible consequences?
4. Does it create interesting conflict rather than an obvious optimal choice?
5. Does the world feel like it continues existing independently of the player?
6. Is the system feasible for a small/indie development scope?

The target is not merely a shop management game with an RPG attached.

The goal is:

> **A game where commerce itself becomes the mechanism through which the player changes people, the town, and the fate of the dungeon.**
