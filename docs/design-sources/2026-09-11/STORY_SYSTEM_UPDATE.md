# Merchan Project

## Story / Time / Information / Ownership System Update

> Status: Current design direction
> Purpose: Update the existing Merchan game design context with newly agreed story foundations and world/time simulation principles.

---

# 1. Dungeon Origin

The origin of dungeons should **not be explained**.

In this world, dungeons are simply a mysterious phenomenon that occasionally occurs.

A dungeon may suddenly appear in:

* a cave
* ruins
* underground structures
* other natural or artificial locations

There is no need to provide a scientific, magical, religious, or historical explanation for why dungeons exist.

The dungeon itself is intentionally supernatural and unexplained.

This is also useful for future sequels.

A future Merchan game could take place in:

* another town
* another country
* another dungeon
* another time period

without requiring all dungeons to be connected to one central origin story.

---

# 2. Dungeon Lifecycle

General world rule:

A dungeon occasionally appears somewhere in the world.

Once it appears, it remains active until someone reaches and conquers its deepest level.

After conquest, the dungeon disappears or becomes permanently inactive.

The exact visual/mechanical behavior after conquest can be decided later.

Eventually, another dungeon may appear elsewhere in the world.

The current game's dungeon is therefore not necessarily unique.

---

# 3. The Protagonist Did Not Cause the Dungeon

This should now be treated as the preferred direction.

The protagonist:

* did not summon the dungeon
* did not activate it
* did not make a magical mistake
* was not chosen by the dungeon

He was simply present when the dungeon appeared.

The tragedy was fundamentally bad luck.

However, this does not prevent the protagonist from developing guilt about what happened.

Personal guilt does not need to correspond to objective responsibility.

---

# 4. Childhood Setup

The protagonist grows up in a small rural town.

Near the town is an ordinary cave.

The protagonist frequently plays there with a childhood friend.

The cave is initially completely ordinary.

The children use it as a place for:

* exploring
* pretending to be adventurers
* hiding
* collecting stones
* playing games

The childhood friend comes from a somewhat wealthier family than the protagonist.

This should not make the friend an unpleasant character.

They are genuine friends.

However, the friend sometimes possesses things the protagonist cannot afford and may innocently show them off.

Example:

The friend owns a small real iron sword.

It is not an important magical weapon.

It is simply something a child would consider extremely impressive.

Example dialogue tone:

> "Look. It's real iron."

> "My father gave it to me."

> "It's not a wooden sword."

The protagonist is somewhat jealous of it.

This establishes the difference between:

**emotional value**
and
**market value**

before the merchant systems even begin.

---

# 5. Dungeon Appearance Incident

While the protagonist and childhood friend are inside the cave, the dungeon suddenly appears.

There is no warning and no explanation.

The cave transforms.

Possible effects:

* corridors change
* space expands unnaturally
* unfamiliar creatures appear
* familiar paths disappear
* the environment becomes dangerous

The children attempt to escape.

The protagonist survives.

The childhood friend does not.

---

# 6. The Friend Is Confirmed Dead

The childhood friend should now be considered dead.

Avoid the later reveal:

> "They were alive at the bottom of the dungeon all along."

That would turn the core story into a conventional rescue plot.

Preferred sequence:

The protagonist escapes.

Later, either alone or as part of the search, the protagonist returns.

The friend's body is found inside the dungeon.

There is therefore no uncertainty regarding whether the friend survived.

The tragedy is permanent.

One of the long-term themes of the story is:

> **Some things cannot be restored.**

---

# 7. The First Important Player Choice: The Body

When the protagonist finds the childhood friend's body, control should return to the player.

The body can be examined.

Possible items:

* iron sword
* small leather pouch
* a few coins
* personal trinket
* possibly food or another ordinary possession

The player can choose whether to take these items.

Possible actions:

* take nothing
* take only the sword
* take only some items
* take everything

The game should NOT display:

> "This is stealing."

or:

> "This is an evil choice."

The game simply allows the action.

This is intended to be one of the first thematic moments of Merchan.

---

# 8. The Iron Sword

The friend's iron sword should be intentionally ordinary.

Example:

**Iron Sword**

* Attack: low / ordinary
* Sell value: 18G

No curse.

No legendary history.

No hidden magical power.

From the game's economic perspective:

> it is worth 18G.

From the friend's perspective:

> it was a treasured possession.

From the protagonist's perspective:

> it represents jealousy, friendship, guilt, death, and childhood.

This item can potentially remain in the player's inventory or storage for the entire game.

Possible later actions:

* keep it
* use it
* sell it
* return it to the friend's family
* display it
* lose it
* potentially pass it to another person

The game should not assign a morality label to any of these actions.

---

# 9. Family and Town Response

The friend's family and the town eventually discover:

* the dungeon
* the child's death
* the body

Therefore, the protagonist does NOT need to hide the dungeon.

The dungeon becoming known is natural.

The friend's family retrieves or buries the body.

If the protagonist took an item before the body was recovered, it may simply remain unnoticed.

For example, the family may focus on the death itself and not immediately notice that a small sword or pouch is missing.

Later dialogue can create emotional pressure without explicitly accusing the protagonist.

Example:

> "He really loved that little sword."

If the sword is still in the player's inventory, that line gains additional meaning.

---

# 10. The Dungeon Is Initially Considered Worthless

The town knows the dungeon exists.

However:

> **A dungeon existing does not automatically mean people will travel there.**

In this world, some dungeons may be:

* extremely dangerous
* poorly explored
* low-value
* already depleted
* too remote
* economically unprofitable

Adventurers therefore consider:

**risk vs reward**

before traveling.

Initially, the new dungeon is known mainly as:

> the dangerous cave where a child died.

There is no reason for elite adventurers to travel to this remote rural town yet.

---

# 11. The Protagonist Begins Returning to the Dungeon

As the protagonist grows older, he continues thinking about the dungeon.

His initial reason does not need to be profit.

Possible motivations include:

* understanding the place connected to his friend's death
* trying to reach deeper areas
* proving something to himself
* confronting his guilt
* simply being unable to leave it alone

The protagonist is capable of exploring shallow dungeon areas.

However, he is not powerful enough to conquer deep floors.

During these shallow explorations, he begins bringing objects back.

Examples:

* unusual stones
* herbs
* monster parts
* old coins
* minerals
* minor relics
* unidentified objects

At first, these are simply things he finds.

---

# 12. Commerce Begins Gradually

The protagonist discovers that some dungeon objects can be sold.

The progression should feel small and organic.

Example:

Find stone
→ sell for 4G

Find strange herb
→ sell for 7G

Find animal-like fang
→ local craftsman buys it

The protagonist gradually realizes:

> "Things from this dungeon have value."

This becomes the beginning of his merchant life.

The protagonist does not need to begin the game as an established professional merchant.

His relationship with commerce can grow directly out of the dungeon.

---

# 13. The Item That Changes the Town

At some point, the protagonist sells an item that appears relatively ordinary to him.

Example:

**Blue Ore**

The protagonist sells it locally for perhaps 35G.

It later passes through:

* another trader
* a craftsman
* a traveling merchant
* a larger market

Eventually, someone outside the town recognizes its true value.

For example:

The ore may be useful for producing unusually powerful weapons or magical equipment.

Rumors spread:

> "A newly formed dungeon in a remote town contains rare material."

This is what begins attracting adventurers.

The protagonist did not deliberately launch a dungeon economy.

It emerges from a normal commercial transaction.

This mirrors one of the central design ideas:

> **The protagonist often creates major consequences by simply buying or selling something.**

---

# 14. Beginning of the Main Game

Suggested structure:

## Prologue

Childhood

→ cave exploration
→ dungeon appears
→ friend dies
→ body discovery
→ optional taking of possessions
→ funeral / aftermath

Then time passes.

## Early Main Game

The protagonist is older.

He returns to the dungeon.

He gathers items.

He sells them.

An unusual item leaves town.

Rumors spread.

The first real adventurers arrive.

This marks the beginning of the town's transformation and the main gameplay loop.

---

# 15. Core Emotional Motivation

The protagonist wants the dungeon conquered.

At least initially, the emotional logic is:

> The dungeon destroyed the peaceful town of his childhood and killed his friend.

The protagonist therefore wants:

> the dungeon gone.

He associates destroying the dungeon with restoring what was lost.

This belief is fundamentally flawed.

Even if the dungeon disappears:

* the friend will remain dead
* the protagonist is no longer a child
* the town will have changed
* new people will live there
* the economy will have transformed
* relationships will have changed

The protagonist must eventually confront:

> **Destroying the dungeon cannot restore the past.**

---

# 16. Central Story Irony

To destroy the dungeon, the protagonist needs stronger adventurers.

To attract stronger adventurers, the protagonist needs a stronger town.

To create a stronger town, the protagonist must expand the dungeon economy.

Therefore:

> **The harder the protagonist works to restore the old town, the more permanently he destroys the old town.**

The town changes from:

small rural town

→ dungeon settlement

→ adventurer destination

→ major economic center

Potential consequences:

Positive:

* better healthcare
* better roads
* more employment
* schools
* shops
* wealth
* safer infrastructure

Negative:

* higher rents
* crime
* gambling
* violence
* black markets
* displacement
* inequality
* cultural loss

The game should not frame growth as purely good or bad.

---

# 17. Core Theme

Current strongest thematic statement:

> **How far are you willing to endanger other people in pursuit of your own goal?**

The protagonist wants the dungeon conquered.

But doing so means encouraging other people to enter it.

The protagonist can:

* finance them
* sell them weapons
* provide medicine
* give them information
* encourage deeper exploration
* reward risky expeditions
* favor particularly useful adventurers

Some will:

* succeed
* become heroes
* become rich
* become injured
* become traumatized
* hate the protagonist
* die

The protagonist rarely kills them directly.

But his actions can make their decisions possible.

---

# 18. Ownership as a Major Theme

The childhood body scene should establish another core question:

> **When does an object become "merchandise"?**

Examples later in the game:

An adventurer dies in the dungeon.

The player discovers:

* sword
* armor
* potion
* personal letter
* valuables

Who owns those items?

Possible answers:

* the dead adventurer
* the family
* the party
* the guild
* the person who found them
* nobody

The law and social expectations may not always agree.

Example:

Dungeon custom:

> "Equipment on a dead adventurer belongs to whoever retrieves it."

This may be legal.

But later:

> "That was my husband's sword. Could I have it back?"

The item may be worth 8,000G.

Returning it produces no direct financial return.

Keeping or selling it is legal.

The game should not explicitly declare one choice correct.

---

# 19. The Childhood Sword as a Thematic Echo

The childhood friend's 18G iron sword should establish a pattern that repeats throughout the game.

Early game:

**Friend's Iron Sword**
Market value: 18G

Late game:

**Famous Adventurer's Sword**
Market value: 50,000G

The underlying question is identical:

> **Is this an item, or does its history create another kind of value?**

This should connect directly to the larger Item Consequence System.

---

# 20. Time Progression

The entire world should share a single continuous timeline.

Whether the protagonist is:

* in town
* inside the shop
* traveling
* inside the dungeon

time continues to pass for everyone.

The protagonist does not freeze the world by entering the dungeon.

---

# 21. Daily World Tick

The current preferred simulation structure is:

> **The world processes a major update once per in-game day.**

At the end of each day, the game runs a Daily World Tick.

This can occur behind a short fade / blackout / day transition.

Example:

Day 31 ends.

Screen fades.

Daily simulation is processed.

Day 32 begins.

The transition should feel like part of the world, not a loading screen.

---

# 22. Day Transition Presentation

## In Town

Possible presentation:

evening

→ shops close

→ town becomes dark

→ fade

→ day number changes

→ morning

## In Dungeon

Possible presentation:

campfire

→ resting

→ darkness

→ fade

→ day number changes

→ next dungeon day

The underlying Daily World Tick is the same in both cases.

---

# 23. Time Continues While the Player Is in the Dungeon

The protagonist may remain inside the dungeon for multiple days.

Example:

Day 30:
Protagonist enters dungeon.

Day 31:
Still exploring.

Day 32:
Still exploring.

Day 33:
Returns to town.

During those days:

* adventurers are moving
* parties are fighting
* people are dying
* shops are operating
* prices are changing
* crimes are happening
* buildings are being constructed
* NPC relationships are changing
* item consequences are progressing

The protagonist simply does not know about them yet.

---

# 24. World Truth vs Player Knowledge

This should be a foundational technical/design concept.

The game should separate:

## World Truth

What has actually happened.

from:

## Player Knowledge

What the protagonist currently knows.

Example:

World Truth:

> Silver Hawk Party reached Floor 30 on Day 32.

Player Knowledge:

> Silver Hawk Party departed six days ago. Current status unknown.

This separation should apply to many systems.

---

# 25. Events Do Not Equal Information

Important rule:

> **An event occurring does NOT automatically mean the player learns about it.**

Example:

Day 40:
A murder occurs.

Day 40:
The protagonist is inside the dungeon.

Day 42:
The murderer escapes.

Day 44:
The protagonist returns.

Only then does the protagonist hear:

> "Something terrible happened while you were gone."

Further investigation may later reveal:

> The murder weapon was an item the protagonist previously sold.

Therefore:

**Event Time**
and
**Discovery Time**

can be different.

---

# 26. Adventurer Information Is Also Delayed

The player should generally NOT have real-time GPS-style information about adventurers.

Bad example:

> Silver Hawk Party
> Current Location: Floor 28
> HP: 61%

Preferred example:

> Silver Hawk Party
> Departed: 5 days ago
> Intended destination: Floor 25+
> Status: Expedition ongoing

The actual internal simulation may know exactly what is happening.

The protagonist does not.

---

# 27. Adventurer Reports Require Return or Discovery

If an adventurer party reaches a major floor:

Example:

Day 32:
Silver Hawk Party reaches Floor 30.

The town does not automatically know.

If they return on Day 36:

Only then may the town learn:

> **Silver Hawk becomes the first party to reach Floor 30.**

Therefore achievement announcements may represent events that happened several days earlier.

---

# 28. Missing Adventurers

Delayed information creates important narrative possibilities.

Example:

Day 20:
Black Wolf Party enters dungeon.

Day 21:
One member dies.

Day 22:
Survivors reach Floor 36.

Day 23:
Entire party dies.

Internally, the game knows this.

The town does not.

Their public status remains:

> Expedition ongoing.

After expected return date:

> Overdue.

Later another party discovers:

* abandoned camp
* weapon
* body
* journal

Only then does the town begin learning what happened.

This uncertainty is desirable.

---

# 29. Information Sources

Different kinds of information should have different delivery methods.

Possible sources:

### Town Events

Learned quickly upon returning to town.

Examples:

* fire
* major crime
* new building
* public death

### Adventurer Reports

Usually known only after someone returns.

### Rumors

Taverns, merchants, travelers.

May be:

* incomplete
* late
* exaggerated
* incorrect

### Guild Information

More reliable, potentially delayed.

### Newspaper / Bulletin Board

Formalized public information.

### Personal Conversations

Important private details.

### Physical Evidence

Items, bodies, journals, equipment.

Information itself can therefore become part of gameplay.

---

# 30. Suggested Player-Facing Expedition Status

Avoid showing hidden simulation state.

Preferred visible fields:

**Silver Hawk Party**

* departed: 6 days ago
* planned duration: 5–8 days
* intended area: Floor 25+
* members: 4
* latest confirmed status: unknown
* expected return: Day 48–51

This creates tension.

Example:

Day 53.

They still have not returned.

The player does not know whether they are:

* progressing successfully
* trapped
* injured
* dead
* intentionally exploring longer

# 32. One World Clock

Design principle:

> **Town time and dungeon time are the same time.**

There should not be:

"town turns"

and separate

"dungeon turns"

that exist independently.

A day spent underground is a day lost above ground.

This creates meaningful opportunity cost.

Examples:

Spend four days exploring:

* gain rare materials
* discover dungeon information

but:

* shop operated without you / remained closed
* customers may go elsewhere
* town events happen without you
* an important adventurer may depart
* prices may change
* a crisis may happen

The player should sometimes have to decide:

> "Do I stay one more day underground?"

without knowing what is currently happening at home.

---

# 33. Time Should Reinforce the Theme

The time/information system is not only simulation infrastructure.

It supports the central emotional theme.

When the protagonist funds an adventurer expedition:

the adventurers leave.

The player cannot watch them.

Days pass.

They fail to return.

The player waits.

This creates the feeling:

> **I sent these people into danger. Now I do not know if they are alive.**

That is much more appropriate to Merchan than directly controlling every expedition.

---

# 34. Relationship to Item Consequence System

The same delayed-information structure should apply to products.

Example:

Day 20:
Player sells strange sword.

Day 23:
Owner begins behaving differently.

Day 26:
Owner commits violence.

Day 27:
Owner disappears.

Day 30:
Player returns from dungeon.

Day 31:
Player learns about the crime.

Day 33:
Player discovers that the weapon used was the sword he sold.

This allows consequences to feel like part of a living world rather than scripted immediate responses.

---

# 35. Current High-Level Story Flow

Current working structure:

### Childhood

Ordinary rural town.

Ordinary cave.

Childhood friendship.

Adventure play.

Dungeon suddenly appears.

Friend dies.

Player discovers body.

First ownership choice.

### Years Later

The protagonist remains obsessed with the dungeon.

Explores shallow areas.

Collects strange materials.

Begins selling them.

Learns to make money from the dungeon.

### Discovery

One seemingly ordinary item is recognized outside the town as valuable.

Rumors spread.

Adventurers begin arriving.

### Growth

The protagonist begins:

* trading
* supporting adventurers
* financing exploration
* developing the town

The dungeon economy expands.

### Conflict

The protagonist wants the dungeon gone.

But everything required to destroy it transforms the town.

His commercial decisions begin producing increasingly large consequences.

### Long-Term Direction

The dungeon is eventually conquered.

However:

* the friend remains dead
* the original town no longer exists
* the protagonist cannot return to childhood
* the town may now depend economically on the dungeon

The final challenge is not merely defeating the dungeon.

It is accepting:

> **The past cannot be restored, and the future must be chosen.**

# 36. Current Core Design Principles

Treat the following as high-priority design principles.

1. The dungeon's origin does not need an explanation.

2. The protagonist did not cause the dungeon.

3. The childhood friend is genuinely dead.

4. Loss should not be undone with a simple rescue reveal.

5. The dungeon is publicly known from early on.

6. Its economic value is initially unknown.

7. Commerce grows naturally out of dungeon exploration.

8. A normal sale can create enormous unintended consequences.

9. Objects have both financial value and personal/history-based value.

10. The game should frequently question ownership.

11. Avoid explicit morality meters.

12. Avoid telling players which choices are morally correct.

13. The entire world shares one continuous timeline.

14. World events continue while the protagonist is absent.

15. World Truth and Player Knowledge must be separate concepts.

16. Adventurer status should generally not be known in real time.

17. Information should require a believable route to reach the protagonist.

18. A day spent in the dungeon is also a day passing in town.

19. The world should feel capable of changing without waiting for the player.

20. Commerce, time, information, town growth, and dungeon progress should be tightly interconnected.

# 39. Instruction to Codex

Treat this document as an updated design context.

Do not implement every detail immediately.

Before writing major systems:

1. Compare this document against the current Merchan codebase.
2. Identify existing systems that already support these concepts.
3. Identify conflicts between the current implementation and this direction.
4. Preserve existing useful functionality where possible.
5. Propose architecture changes before performing large rewrites.
6. Keep World State and Player Knowledge separate in future data design.
7. Avoid hardcoding major narrative consequences directly into UI logic.
8. Design event and information systems so delayed discovery is possible.

The target experience is:

> **A world that continues moving while the merchant is absent, where the player changes that world through commerce without always knowing the consequences until much later.**
